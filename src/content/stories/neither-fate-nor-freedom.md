The court convened at four in the morning.

No jury. Only a row of empty chairs, names pasted to the backs: Einstein, Bohr, Camus, Laplace. The last name had been torn off, leaving a ring of glue. A figure sat on the bench, face cut in two by the lamp's shade, unreadable. I sat in the defendant's box.

The judge struck the gavel. The gavel made no sound.

"You are charged with freedom," said the prosecutor.

He was tall, in a black coat with no buttons, holding a ledger. On its cover: *The Position and Momentum of All Particles*. He did not need to open it. He knew every page.

"The charge is not that you did evil," he said. "It is that you believe you chose something."

I laughed. The laugh bounced off the empty chairs and came back to my feet.

"Do you have evidence?"

"I do." He opened the ledger. "First: your brain decided before you knew you had decided. The instruments recorded the readiness potential before you raised your hand. You think the raising was yours. The signal came first; the explanation came after. Second: the desires you call your own were borrowed. The things you long for — advertising wrote their adjectives for you. The things you fear — school drew their borders. Third: you were born into a particular era, a particular language, a particular family, and those conditions set the limit of what you are able to think. You did not choose your mother tongue, and you did not choose your questions."

"All of that I concede," I said.

"Then you agree."

"Conceding is not confessing."

"In a determinist court, conceding is confessing," he said. "You are not responsible for your acts. You are not responsible for your thoughts. You are one segment of a chain. A chain does not judge itself."

In the gallery, Einstein raised a hand and lowered it. Bohr sat on the other side, head down, as if smiling. Camus had not come. Laplace's chair stood empty, but its seat held a thin layer of dust, as though someone had just risen from it.

The judge spoke. The voice came out of my own throat.

"Defendant, you may make a statement."

"It is true I did not choose my mother tongue," I said. "I did not choose which fear raised me. But I am speaking now. This act of speaking is not in your ledger. You can predict my next sentence. You cannot say it for me."

"I can," the prosecutor said. "Your rebuttal is in the ledger too. I know on which page you will grow angry, on which page you will tire, on which page you will quote Camus."

"Then why come at all?"

"Because persuasion is part of the chain," he said. "You think you are rebutting me. You are completing a rebuttal written long ago. The fiercer you get, the more you prove the rebuttal is yours. The harder you try to prove freedom, the more you prove that the concept of freedom is leading you by the hand."

I looked at him. His face was like paper folded many times over, and every crease was full of my handwriting.

"You said I did not choose my questions," I said. "Correct. But once a question is asked, it leaves a hole in the system. You can explain how the hole got there. You cannot explain why the hole hurts."

"Pain is also an electrical signal."

"Yes. But an electrical signal does not ask itself why it hurts."

"How do you know?"

"Because one is asking right now."

The prosecutor was silent for a second. A short second. A needle dropped on carpet. But I heard it.

The empty chairs in the gallery began to creak, faintly. Not wind. Someone sitting, standing, sitting again. The names peeled off the chair backs one by one, like dried labels. Einstein, Bohr, Camus, Laplace — none of them were witnesses. They were the audience I had summoned for my own question.

"Call the future," said the judge.

A small door at the end of the courtroom opened. No one came through. A shaft of light fell on the floor, shaped like a person. The prosecutor rose and addressed the light. "What do you see?"

A voice came out of the light. My voice, a little older.

"I see him sitting in the same chair," the voice said. "I see him ask the same question three times. At sixteen. At twenty-five. At forty. Each time he believes he is choosing. Each time he chooses the same road. In the end he will become the kind of person he feared, and the kind of person he envied. The two kinds are one person."

"Do you hear?" The prosecutor turned to me. "The future has testified."

"I hear," I said. "But when the future testifies, it uses my voice. Which means the future is not a witness. The future is another me, sitting in another chair, speaking on my behalf."

"Is there a difference?"

"Yes," I said. "A witness can leave the courtroom. Another me cannot. He can only look at it from the inside."

The judge pushed the lampshade up a little. The shadow receded to my feet. The face still did not show itself fully, but I saw an old scar on the jaw. My own jaw.

"Do you believe the future is already written?" the judge asked me.

"Sometimes," I said. "If I know what I will become, I can spend a year idle, a year taking the long way round, and I will still arrive there. The future is like a basin. Every path bends inward. Past and future are not a straight line either — more like a Möbius strip. You think you are walking forward. You are arriving at the reverse side."

"Then why keep walking?"

"Because I do not know the shape of the basin," I said. "I can only walk from inside it."

"Is that freedom?"

"No."

"Then what is it?"

"Luck," I said. "A little white space at the edge of the text. An actor, between fixed lines, deciding whether this one comes out smiling or with teeth clenched. The script does not change. The tone changes. Tone does not change the ending, but it changes who I am. At least it changes who I am in this minute."

"You know this is not freedom," the prosecutor said.

"I know."

"You know it is only an interpretation."

"I know."

"You know interpretations are also decided."

"That too," I said. "But if even the sentence 'I know' is decided, then whatever decides it must first let me know. Knowing is a surplus movement inside the system. A surplus movement does not overturn the chain, but it gives the chain one more observer. The observer cannot exit the chain. The observer can stand inside it and ask one question: 'Who is asking?'"

The empty chairs all turned toward me. The shade over the bench swayed. The face cut by shadow finally lifted.

It was my face.

"Verdict," said the judge.

"Guilty," said the prosecutor.

"Of what?"

"No charge can be brought," said the judge. "He is not free. He is not determined either. He is the one who, between the two accounts, chooses the tone in which to go on living."

The gavel fell. Still no sound.

I walked out of the courtroom. The sky had not brightened. On the asphalt lay the print of a tire, like a verdict with no signature on it. I stood beside that verdict for a while. Wind came from a far-off city, carrying rain and iron.

I had not won. I had not lost.

I was only still walking.
