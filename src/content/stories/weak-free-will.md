The night train pulled out at one in the morning.

No one spoke above a murmur. Every head was down, every face lit the same color by a phone screen. The blue glow rose off the rows of seats like a thin film of water. When the train entered a tunnel, two things appeared in the glass at once: the lights slipping past outside, and the rows of lit, expressionless faces inside, all carrying the same reflected shine. Under the tunnel noise you could hear the small sounds of a crowd not talking — a thumbnail tapping glass, a cough held until it passed, a bag shifted from one shoulder to the other, the seat springs creaking as someone leaned away from the person beside them.

I walked along the aisle.

A young man looked up. His eyes were bright — too bright to have been reading a screen.

"Can't sleep either?" he said.

"I'm looking for someone," I said. "Someone who can send a sentence back to where it came from."

"Which sentence?"

"The one you just said to yourself."

He smiled. When he smiled, his mouth moved first and his eyes followed a beat later, like a video on the wrong playback speed. He let the smile sit there, waiting to see what I would do with it.

"How do you know I said anything?"

"Because you looked up," I said. "People with their heads down don't look up — unless a sentence has just hit them."

He didn't deny it. He turned his phone face-down on his knee. Light leaked between his fingers and touched a patch of skin on the back of his hand, and for a second his fingers curled to cover it, as if the glow were something he'd been caught holding.

"Fine," he said. "The sentence was: everything is discipline. You think you're choosing, but you're only repeating the choices you were taught."

"Who said that?"

"Don't know."

"What year?"

"Don't know."

"Who was he answering?"

"Don't know."

"If it's wrong, what evidence would you need to find out?"

He was quiet for a long time. The train took a curve, and the car tilted gently, the way a person tips their head when they're thinking.

> You're asking too much.
>
> A sentence only has to land and you're done. That's enough. You need it. You need a name for the weight you're carrying.

"That isn't my voice," I said.

"It's the voice inside you," he said. "Words in the head don't need an author."

I looked at him. His coat cuffs were worn white. His nails were cut short. He resembled someone from years ago. Maybe he resembled me. He looked back like he was turning the same thought over and hadn't decided what to do with it yet.

"Then let's run an experiment," I said. "Take the sentence apart. The first word: everything. What's in everything? This train you're sitting on? The reason you just looked up? The fact that you believe it?"

He opened his mouth and closed it.

> Pull it apart like that and you can pull apart anything.
>
> Take it all apart, and what am I supposed to live on?

"Your own life," I said. "Not a sentence."

The screens in the car lit up together. A notification dropped in like a pebble in a pond, and the ripple spread across every face. Someone laughed. Someone frowned. Someone pressed their phone closer. The young man put his head down. His face went blue again, and the expression that had been rising in it went out the way a match goes out — not slowly, but all at once. I could have stayed. There was more to say. But the aisle was clear, and the other cars were waiting.

I kept walking.

At the coupling between cars stood an old man holding a ticket with no seat number. When he saw me, he held it up like a verdict.

"You here for the lecture too?" he asked.

"What lecture?"

"Every station, someone gets on and gives one," he said. "Last stop it was alienation. The stop before, the absurd. All correct. I listen, I think, yes, that's me. And then the doors open and it's my stop, and I still have to go back to work."

> Philosophy is a luxury.
>
> You need spare time before you can think about this. People without spare time aren't refusing to think. They don't have the energy.

"You disagree?" the old man asked.

"I disagree with calling 'no energy' 'no ability,'" I said. "But I know that a hungry man won't stop to ask who wrote a slogan. That's not his fault, and I won't tell him it is. It's why slogans spread. A slogan doesn't need you to understand it. It needs you to repeat it."

The old man nodded, then shook his head. He folded the ticket along the same crease it had already been folded on. He put it in his pocket. The intercom crackled.

"Next stop, discipline. Next stop, discipline. Passengers alighting, please prepare."

No one moved. The doors opened on a platform I couldn't see from where I stood, and nobody in the car so much as lifted their head to check what was out there.

I went to the last car. There were no seats here, only a window. Outside it was black; inside it was my face. I looked at myself. My head was down too. My hand held a screen too. On the screen were sentences someone else had written — short, forceful, almost true.

> See. You're on the train too.
>
> You think standing in the aisle makes you clearer than the rest. You're just standing taller. You're looking at the same screen.

"I know," I said.

"Know what?"

"I know I've read a few more books than they have. A few more books isn't a second life. I can describe myself in prettier words, and describe other people in prettier words. Pretty words don't change the train. They change my posture in it — the angle of my chin, the way I fold my arms. In a car full of people with their heads down, that's enough to be mistaken for standing."

My face shifted in the glass, like water under wind. The lights dimmed a notch. The intercom spoke again.

"Next stop, weak free will."

I pressed the door release. The door opened. The platform was empty — one lamp, one bench. On the bench sat a teenager in a school uniform, head down, holding a screen. He looked up at me the way the young man had. His uniform was clean and ironed, his shoes scuffed at the toes, and he sat with his bag balanced on his knees like a weight he'd been told to carry without complaint.

"Getting off?" he asked.

"Yes."

"There's nothing here."

"I know."

"Then why?"

I stood in the doorway. The doors began to close. The train hadn't moved yet. The platform lamp came in and lit a strip of floor between us. The strip was bare. Nothing written on it. I could feel the train's engines building behind me, a hum that hadn't become motion, the way a held breath isn't yet a word.

"Because between the door opening and the door closing, there's one second," I said. "In that second the train hasn't left, and I haven't gone back. No one liked it, no one argued, no one offered me a better version. I'm just standing inside one second. That second is mine."

The doors met. The train pulled away. The lamp on the platform stayed lit. The boy lowered his head, and the screen lit his face again.

I stood on the empty platform and heard the rails humming in the distance. I wasn't any freer. I had one more second. That second was so short it couldn't change anything.

But in that second, I didn't say anyone's words.
