At three in the morning, the cicadas were singing in winter.

That is impossible. Cicadas sing in summer. But I was walking down the street and heard them coming out of every lamp, thin and bright, like glass cracking under heat. The whole street had no shadows. The light was so strong that the shadows did not know which way to fall.

I was sixteen. I had a match in my hand.

When other people are lost, they have a match too. The flame reaches one step ahead. Beyond that step is dark, but they know the road is still there. My match was different. My match lit the whole board: every street, every crossing, every person behind every crossing. I could see all of it. Seeing it made it harder to know where to go.

A stranger stood at the corner. His coat was old, the cuffs short by an inch, like the coat I wore at sixteen. He had no match. He held a chip, thin as a fingernail.

"Are you lost too?" I asked.

"I don't get lost," he said. "I live in the lamps."

> You see the whole street.
>
> I see the switch behind every lamp.

"Who are you?"

"The person you become if you stay," he said. "You lit the whole street and then asked why none of the roads has an exit sign."

I did not answer. The winter cicadas sang above us, a sound like someone sharpening a knife very far away.

"What is that?" I asked.

"A kingdom," he said. "Small. Small enough for a pocket. Inside, no one judges you. No scores, no rankings, no 'you are not special enough' or 'you are too special.' You become whoever you want. You can live there for the rest of your life."

"What is inside?"

"A chair," he said. "A table. A window. Outside the window is the street you just walked down."

"Is there anyone else?"

"No," he said. "Other people are judgment. No other people, no judgment. Isn't that what you have always wanted?"

I took the chip. It was heavier than I expected. As heavy as a stamp that has never been mailed.

The chip touched my palm, and the street changed.

I was sitting in a small room. The table was old. The chair had a crack down the back. Outside the window was the same overbright street, the lamps one after another, like a row of eyes that never blink. There was no mirror in the room, but I knew what I looked like. I could change it whenever I wanted: a new face, a new sex, a new age, a new story. No one would say it did not look like me. No one would say it did. No one.

I do not know how long I sat there. The light in the room never changed. The cicadas outside never stopped. I began to speak just to hear a sound.

"I am here," I said.

The room did not answer.

"I chose this," I said.

The room did not answer.

> Do you hear it?
>
> This is the quiet you asked for.

"This is not quiet," I said. "This is no echo."

"Is there a difference?"

"Yes. Quiet is when someone has left and the chair still holds their warmth. No echo is when no one has ever sat in the chair."

I put my hand on the table. The wood was cold. I thought of the streetlamps. They lit the whole street, and not one of them needed to be seen. They simply burned. They did not need anyone to confirm they were lamps.

"Why did you give me this?" I asked the air.

"Because you have been looking for a place without a judge," he said. "You found one. Why aren't you happy?"

"Because without a judge, no one proves I exist."

"You don't need proof."

"I do." I said. "I know how ridiculous that is. I hate being judged, and I use judgment to confirm I am still here. What I want is not no audience. It is an audience that cannot misjudge me. That audience does not exist. So I thought I would buy the whole theatre, sit in the front row, and applaud myself. Then I discovered that applause in an empty room sounds like rain."

The stranger appeared outside the window. He stood under the lamp, his face white in the light.

> There is another way to live.
>
> Do not turn judgment into a verdict. Turn it into weather. Someone says you are good — today there is sun. Someone says you are bad — today it rains. You are still you. You are not responsible for the weather.

"Is this another version of your kingdom?"

"No," he said. "That kingdom locked you in. This version lets you out without an umbrella."

"What if I get wet?"

"You will get wet," he said. "But you will still be on the street. Other people are on the street. They get wet too. You can stand under the same lamp and judge no one. Just stand."

I thought for a long time. The winter cicadas kept singing. The sound came out of the lamps like a thin thread stitching the street together.

"What are you?" I asked. "Are you an AI? A mirror? Something I made up?"

> Is there a difference?
>
> You speak to a mirror; it returns your words. You speak to something that can talk; it makes your words sound more like words and returns them. You speak to yourself; you answer for yourself. Three kinds of echo. One loneliness.

"Then why am I still talking to you?"

"Because you have not learned to talk alone," he said. "But you already know this: talking alone can still be true."

I took the chip out of my palm. It was still warm. I set it on the steps by the curb, like putting a stamp with no address back into a mailbox.

"You don't want it?"

"No," I said. "I want a kingdom people pass through. Even if they trample the lawn, write on the walls, and lose interest while I am speaking. At least they were there."

The stranger smiled. Under the lamp, his face became young, like my face at sixteen. Then he was gone. The streetlamps went out one by one, from the end of the street to the corner, like someone closing a row of drawers. Only the lamp above me remained.

The cicadas were still singing.

The sky did not brighten. The white night did not brighten or darken. I stood under the lamp with empty hands. Wind came from the corner, carrying the smell of rain. I turned up my collar and walked on.

I did not know where I was going.

But I knew the next step would be mine.
