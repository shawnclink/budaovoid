The guild hall convenes at night.

Empty chairs on both sides of a long table. No papers on it. Only a glass hive. Inside, the bees move, wings knocking against glass with a thin, steady sound. An old map hangs on the wall, its borders faded, as if someone has wiped them many times.

I sit at one end of the table. At the other end sits an old man. He wears the clothes of the nineteenth century, his beard long, the snows of Siberia in his eyes. An old, worn book rests in front of him. The title is *Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution*.

"You came," he says.

"I came."

"How many did you bring?"

"One. Me."

"Enough. A court needs plaintiff, defendant, witness. One person can be all three."

The door opens. A figure in dark clothes steps in, holding no book, only a ruler. The ruler has no markings.

"I am the Measure," he says. "I have a question. Can your mutual aid hold a city?"

The old man pushes the book to the middle of the table.

"Not the city yet. Ask the bee first. There is no king in a hive. The queen does not rule. She only lays. The workers decide for themselves where to forage, when to split. No central plan, no bureaucracy, no police. What do they rely on? Signals. Antennae. Pheromones. One touch after another. A hive survives because it holds a mass of individuals that trade information directly."

"Bees number in the tens of thousands," the Measure says. "A city numbers in the millions."

"Which is why I said, not the city yet. Ask instead: why does an organization need a middle layer?"

He looks at me. I know he is waiting.

"Out of distrust. Because those above don't believe those below can understand. Because information deforms on its way down. A decision clear at the top arrives in the middle as three sentences, and at the bottom as a slogan. Most of what the middle layer does is not creation but correction. It bends the deformed information back, blocks the misreading, watches the people who carry out orders."

"And if nothing deforms?"

"Then the middle layer is only cost. Two layers: decision and execution. Everyone who decides gives everyone who executes the full reason. Not the conclusion, the reason. The reason arrives, and the executor judges for himself. No one stands in between judging for them."

The Measure taps the table. Soft. But the bees in the hive stop for one second.

"Pretty words. Now the city. A city holds different communities, different interests, different tongues. Two communities both think they are right. Who rules between them? A street must be torn down. The people who live on it say no. The people next door say yes. Who decides? Can your two layers bring everyone to one table? And if they come, can they agree in a single night? If they can't, do you keep talking, or does someone have to be forced to accept? If someone is forced, where is your principle of no coercion? If no one is forced, how do cities build roads, lay drains, hold back plague?"

The old man says nothing.

"And transition. You live now in a world with a state, with police, with law. You want to get to a world without them. How do you walk that road? Who presides over the crossing? Whoever presides holds power. He holds the most power at the moment you most need him to let it go. What do you do? Vote him out? The vote is itself a machine of the state. Overthrow him? Whoever overthrows him also holds the gun."

The hall is quiet. The bees begin to move again. The sound is like rain far away.

"You are right," the old man says. "I have no answer."

"You admit it?"

"I admit it. I observed small groups. A village, a guild, a herd. I can prove that mutual aid is an advantage in small groups. I cannot prove it scales to a city of millions. Anyone who says otherwise is lying to you. The anarchist experiments of history were all short and small. Not one long, large-scale sample."

The Measure turns to me.

"And you? You have no answer either?"

"No complete one. But I have three incomplete ones."

"Say them."

"First, the city is not a giant hive. It is a network. A network needs no center. It needs protocol: who may connect, how, what happens when a link breaks. Protocol is not government, it is rule. Rule can be kept by the people who use it. Second, scale is not one problem. It is many small problems. A city can split into many two-layer organizations. A street, a school, a water plant. Each decides its own affairs. Affairs that cross between them are negotiated federatively. The federation is not a center, it is an ad hoc table. Third, transition is not one revolution. It is many exits. You cannot dismantle the state in a night. You can, in the seams of the state, first build mutual-aid networks that don't depend on it. The network survives first, then grows. The state will not step aside on its own. But it ages, it slows, it leaves empty space where it can't reach."

"Then who runs the network?" the Measure asks. "Who writes the protocol? Who maintains the servers? If one person controls the network, he controls everyone. Your center has changed its name. It is called a platform."

I look at him. His ruler still has no markings.

"You are right. That is the most dangerous place. Lowering the cost of coordination also lowers the cost of control. A mesh network can let people connect directly, and it can let one person see everyone directly. The direction of technology is not fixed. It depends on who owns it, who maintains it, who can fix it when it breaks. If only one company can fix it, it is a state. If every community can fix it, then it is a net."

The old man closes the book. He walks to the glass hive, presses his palm against it. The bees crawl under his palm, like a small patch of living ink.

"Do you know why I wrote *Mutual Aid*?"

"To refute social Darwinism."

"Not only. To prove this: people can live together without fear. That is not a political program. That is a judgment about the human animal. You may reject my program. You cannot reject the judgment. Without it, every system is only another way of watching people."

The Measure sets the ruler down on the table. It meets the wood with a soft click.

"I still don't believe you," he says. "But I grant you the judgment is worth a dinner."

"Then we eat," the old man says. "There are no judges at a dinner table."

The hall's door opens. Outside is a long corridor, and light at its end. The bees keep hitting the glass, patiently. The old man leaves first. The Measure follows, and at the doorway turns back to look at me.

"You'll fail."

"Probably."

"You'll keep going?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because the bees are still hitting the glass. They don't know what is outside it. They only know their wings can still move."

I am the last to leave the guild hall. The corridor is long. Maps on the wall, one after another, and the borders shift under the lamps. I reach the end and push open the next door.

Outside is not a city.

It is a map that no one has finished drawing.
