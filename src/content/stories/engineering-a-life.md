The workshop was still lit at three in the morning.

On the table lay a paper notebook, a pen, and an iPad that had never been switched off. The screen sat on the unlock screen, throwing a small slab of cold light across the desk. At the far end of the table stood a machine with no power running to it: a brass flywheel, a few connecting rods, an empty spring barrel. It looked like something carried out of an old laboratory. It also looked like a skeleton.

I sat down. Someone was already across from me.

He wore the same clothes I wore, only older. There was ink on his fingers, dirt under his nails. He looked at me the way a man looks at something that is not finished.

"You're designing your life again?" he asked.

"Not designing," I said. "Engineering."

"What's the difference?"

"Design is drawing a pretty picture. Engineering is making it keep turning after it breaks."

He smiled. When he smiled, the lines at the corners of his eyes creased like folded paper.

"Then tell me first," he said, "why does this machine need a flywheel?"

"Inertia," I said. "Qian Xuesen writes about systems. A system doesn't run because the people inside it push every day. It runs on its own inertia. Once the flywheel is moving, you don't have to push it every second. It remembers the direction for you. Lives work the same way. A good system accumulates a little each day, and you don't have to be a hero in it. A bad system accumulates a little each day too, in the other direction."

"You talk about a life like it's a machine."

"I'm only borrowing the machine's language," I said. "Machines don't get tired. People do. So a person needs something that turns for him without willpower."

He reached out and touched the flywheel. It did not move. His fingers left a faint streak of dust on the metal.

"The paper and the iPad on your desk," he said. "Which do you choose?"

"Paper."

"Why?"

"Paper is one layer," I said. "I open the notebook, pick up the pen, write. Nothing in between — no unlock, no home screen, no hunting for an app, no waiting for it to respond. The iPad is four layers. Screen, touch layer, system, application. Every layer asks me for one more motion. Every motion costs a little attention. By the end of a day, the layers have eaten it."

"So you treat layers as the enemy."

"Not all layers," I said. "Some layers are structure. You can't delete the compiler. You can't tear down a load-bearing wall. What I delete are the layers that only make friction and make nothing else — a confirmation box, a spare step, a middleman who exists so he can look important. The fewer the layers, the less the intention leaks."

He nodded. He turned the iPad face down. The screen went dark. In the workshop there was only the lamp.

"Is this how you learn things too?" he asked.

"Top-down is slow," I said. "Binary first, then instructions, then language, then program. Every layer asks for your patience before you've seen a single result. I do better the other way. Give me a sorting algorithm first — give me two nested loops and let me watch it put a pile of numbers in order. Then I walk back. I ask why it's written that way, when it breaks, what data structure is under it. That's diffusive learning. The answer first, then take the answer apart. Take it apart until the principle shows itself."

"Sounds like laziness."

"Sounds like walking from where the light is toward where it isn't," I said. "Not everyone can stand in the dark for three years first."

He put his hand on the spring barrel. The barrel was empty.

"And this?" he asked. "What does the machine lack?"

"A mainspring."

"No," he said. "Something that winds the spring for you when you're not here."

I looked at him. His face in the lamplight was calm — calm like a lake with no wind on it.

"You're the one who comes back," I said.

"I'm the one who comes back after you give up," he said. "When you write a plan, you use the best version of you. That one wakes early, has attention to spare, knows what he wants. That one lays out a beautiful week. But he only lives a few days. After a few days, he leaves. I'm the one who comes back. I have no plan, no strength, and no interest in reading the notes you left. The system you designed is for the best version of you. Have you ever thought about leaving me a road?"

The workshop went quiet. The flywheel held a dim gold shine under the lamp.

"I have thought about it," I said. "That's why I say the system has to be able to restart. The cost of a restart can't exceed a few minutes. It can't make me read my own notes first, can't make me forgive myself first, can't make me get back in state first. Better to have one default action. I do that one thing, and the machine turns again."

"What about the machine you have now?"

"It has no mainspring yet."

"Why not wind one in?"

"Because I'm still looking for a spring that won't break," I said. "I've tried many kinds. Every one of them needed me to wind it daily. Winding it daily still means willpower. Willpower still means it snaps."

He stood. He walked to the machine, reached into the empty spring barrel, and turned something inside it. There was nothing there, yet it made a very small sound, like a string plucked once.

"Have you heard the word?" he asked. "Polymath. The one who knows everything. Da Vinci's kind. What you want."

"I've heard it."

"Do you still want it?"

"I do," I said. "But I know it fights with 'reduce friction.' If a person wants to learn many things, he has to keep many entrances. The more entrances, the more friction. You cut layers so you can move faster; you keep layers so you can go farther. You can't do both at once."

"So you have to choose one."

"I don't choose," I said. "I build a system that switches. Most of the time, the narrow gate — a side door when I need it. The narrow gate is daily life. The side door is ambition. The side door can't stay open every day, but it can't be missing."

He looked at me for a long time. Then he smiled. This time he smiled like a man who has finally heard the answer, and also like a man who knows the answer isn't enough.

"You still think too beautifully," he said. "A system isn't a drawing. A system is what you actually do every day. What will you do today?"

"I'll put what I need tomorrow on the desk," I said. "One thing. Not ten. Then I'll go to sleep."

"That's the mainspring?"

"That's the mainspring."

He walked to the door. Outside, the sky had not broken. There was only one thin gray line.

"The machine will stop again," he said.

"I know."

"You'll come back."

"I know."

"When you come back, don't curse yourself."

I did not answer. He pulled the door shut. In the workshop there was only me and the machine with no power in it.

I picked up the pen and wrote one line on the paper:

Tomorrow, do one thing.

Then I set the pen down, shut the iPad off, and turned the lamp down to its lowest. In the dark the flywheel stayed still, like an eye shut.

I know it will stop again tomorrow. And I know that tomorrow I will come back and wind it up again.

Not because I'll become a better person.

Because I've finally started to leave a road for the one who isn't perfect.
