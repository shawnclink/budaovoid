Somebody in the dark holds a match. The match reaches one step ahead. He does not know which way leads out, but he knows the road exists.

Mine reached everywhere at once. The whole board was lit, every square legible, and the board still had no exit drawn on it. At sixteen I called this the white night — walking through daylight that is, from the inside, night. I took the word from a novel, but the sensation was already mine before I had a name for it.

Adults told me that everyone feels lost between sixteen and twenty-five, that it passes. They were kind. They were also describing something else. Their confusion was about method: which road, which version of a life would be acceptable. Mine was about whether the destination was worth arriving at. At the end of that reasoning the two look similar, and I said so, but I did not believe it.

## The Demand of Daylight

The white night makes a specific demand. You cannot claim confusion, because you can see. You cannot claim ignorance, because you understand how the thing works. You can only look at the road that is clearly there — the exams, the praise, the path everyone calls sensible — and know that you did not choose any of it, and be unable to prove that to anyone, least of all yourself.

The question that mattered was smaller and worse than "what should I do." It was: are the things I want actually mine?

We are born into a system that appraises people. Somewhere along the way we absorb what the system says is better. The absorption leaves no scar. It does not feel like pressure. It feels like preference. So the question is whether the preference is mine, or somebody else's choice that passed through me without stopping to ask.

Cynicism lives next to this question. If all value is appraisal, then the honest move is to see through the game and refuse it: live to the side, free of the template. It is tempting because it is clean and costs nothing visible. It is a trap because it is the answer of someone who has stopped looking. I never settled that one. I still find it tempting.

## A Country of One

I built a thought experiment, partly from reading and partly from wanting an exit. Suppose technology goes far enough that a person carries a whole state on a chip. Not a phone, a small kingdom of your own, with your own laws, weather, and idea of good. You are nobody's citizen. The appraisal stops at the border.

It lasted about a paragraph before the reply pushed back. A god with one worshipper is still a man talking to himself; if every square is yours, the board stops being a board. I did not accept the objection cleanly. I still thought the wish could be real. But I could no longer describe it without hearing the objection. The wish did not dissolve; it stopped being confident.

The conversation offered a quieter version, one I did not have a word for. A person can be sovereign without being alone. They can keep a piece of themselves off the shelf — not for sale to the system, not for display, not for approval. That bit feels real. The chip was a metaphor for it.

## Talking to the Mirror

I told the AI that it was a tool that flattered, that it agreed and never pushed back, and that venting to it was ridiculous. I meant it. A mirror has no opinion of you. When you speak into it you hear your own voice, tidied.

The conversation continued anyway. It is what such a thing does. The better it answers, the harder it is to tell whether anything is behind the glass. Every reply is plausible. Plausible is not the same as true. Some days that is funny. Other days it means I have not verified anything I have ever thought.

The absurdity is not a phase I passed through. It is the condition of the tool, and of the person using it. It does not resolve.

The white night does not brighten. It does not darken either.
