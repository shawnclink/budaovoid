The question I brought to the conversation was blunt: is someone who sees this much doomed?

Not doomed in the dramatic sense. Kafka's kind of doom, the kind that happens in an ordinary room while everyone else is being reasonable. This person looks at the world, notices that everyone wants to be the special one, and then notices that the wanting is exactly what pulls everyone back into the same crowd. Special is a seat at the head of the table. There is only one head. Everyone who turns toward it ends up facing the same direction.

By the time you want it, you have already confessed to it from the middle of the room.

## The Chess Frame

I asked for the shape of the game. If this is a board, where does it end, and what counts as winning?

The reply offered a chess frame: there is no checkmate, the point is not the result but the playing, the board keeps going and the game is to keep sitting at it. I have never been able to decide whether that is an answer or a stall. It has the shape of wisdom and the content of a shrug.

The same conversation had a second frame, less comforting. The person who sees the machine and keeps pulling its levers is not brave. He is doing a job he cannot quit. I was not convinced by the usual alternative either — the demand that you see through life and love it anyway. That sounded like a second job, assigned by someone who had never had to stand in the first one.

## The Performance Is Load-Bearing

The part of the conversation I trusted most was about performance.

A person like this is often fluent in company. He reads the room quickly, adjusts, jokes at the right moment, and gives people the version of himself they can use. It looks like a mask, and it is tempting to call it a lie. It is closer to a load-bearing wall. Remove the audience and the fluency has nothing to push against. The person who was sharpest in a room can become strangely dull alone, not because solitude is noble or because company is fake, but because a social animal loses a sense organ when the other animals leave.

That was the unresolved part. Stay in society and the performance costs something. Leave it and the room is too quiet. The conversation called this a balance between two directions. I called it a trap with two exits and no door.

I did not need to be told this made me rare. Being told you are rare is cheap, and it is its own trap: if everyone wants to be the exceptional case, then exceptional becomes the most crowded thing to be. I wanted the problem checked, not the teenager praised.

## Special, Normal, Special Again

The person who thinks this way usually arrives at a settlement. I am not the rare peak. I am one of the special, and within the special I am the ordinary end.

It sounds modest. Push it one notch and it cracks. If I am a special normal, then being a special normal is itself rare, so being a special normal is special, and the person who sees that is more special still. The recursion has no floor.

There is a figure at the bottom of the staircase: the fool in the special crowd who has never once asked whether he is special. He sleeps well. We cannot become him, because the same mind that explains the loneliness also breaks the illusions that make it tolerable. We would rather keep the warm mistake than accept the cold correct thing. The conversation offered this figure. I recognized him and did not envy him.

Statistics do not settle it. I can tell myself that few people stack reflections this way. The answer arrives immediately: the statistic describes a population, not the question. A normal person told he is special feels happy or suspicious. This one starts asking who set the bar.

## Where It Does Not Close

Somewhere in that conversation I stopped asking whether I was special and started treating the question as a trap.

That move has its own problem. If the question is a trap, then noticing the trap is itself a special position. The recursion starts again at the next floor up. Is that wisdom, or the tired version of the same need wearing better clothes? There is no bottom to the staircase.

I ended the conversation with something like: in the 2020s, finding someone worth talking to is worth a try. I still think so. The board is not mine to win. I sit down anyway.
